1. The 3-Part Executive Hook Blueprint First 90 Seconds

Never open a pitch or meeting by describing your tools or asking for favors. Capture the listener's brain with this 3-step sequence:

STEP 1: THE MACRO SHIFT (The World Changed)

"Over the past [6-12 months], the market / operational landscape has shifted fundamentally..."
👉 Establishes common ground and makes the listener nod "yes" in the first 5 seconds.

STEP 2: THE HIGH-STAKES FRICTION (The Pain of the Old Way)

"Yet, most teams are still relying on legacy approaches, leading to [wasted hours / cost / release delays]..."
👉 Highlights the urgent cost of doing nothing. Without friction, executives take no action.

STEP 3: THE VALUE ANCHOR (The Concrete Win)

"Our proposal eliminates this friction and delivers [specific, quantified business outcome]..."
👉 Drops a solid anchor (e.g., "saves 20 hours a week" or "recovers 15% of lost sales").

2. The "Zoom-Out Camera" Secret Scope & Executive Stakes

Why did the manager not care about your Macro Shift? Because it was stuck at desk level! Fly the Drone Camera up into the sky:

LEVEL 1: MY DESK ❌ "I spend too much time on manual Excel entry." (Sounds like personal complaining. Manager ignores.)
LEVEL 2: OUR TEAM ⚠️ "Our team is slow at updating customer records." (A bit better, but still vague.)
LEVEL 3: MACRO HORIZON 💎 "Since our customer accounts doubled to 5,000, our 48-hour data lag creates bottlenecks that put our Q4 renewal targets at risk." (Executive sits up and listens!)
The 3 Executive Levers: An executive only cares if your Macro Shift touches: 1. Company Scale / Volume (accounts doubled, traffic up 70%), 2. Market Expectations (clients demand 24h turnaround), or 3. High-Stakes Deadlines (Q4 release at risk).

4. Vocal Mechanics & Delivery Rules Webcam Presence

1. Downward Declarative Inflection (↘)

Stop ending statements with rising pitch like a question ("We can do this? ↗"). Drop your pitch firmly on the period ("We can do this. ↘"). It creates instant executive gravity.

2. The Consonant Guillotine

Cut off trailing Katakana vowels on stops! Say /pɪtʃ/ (pitch), not pitch-u. Say /ˈklaɪənt/ (client), not clien-to. Lock your tongue to stop the breath dead.

Hi YH. In our first session, we focused on breaking the habit of relying on elementary conversational default verbs (Tier 1) and transitioning decisively to the language used by engineering directors, project leads, and department heads in multinational organizations (Tier 3).

Using words like do, make, big problem, or talk about is grammatically functional, but it conceals your seniority and technical expertise. This worksheet consolidates our 10 core ladders, details the exact preposition rules to prevent Japanese L1 interference, and provides realistic workplace scenarios for you to practice before our next lesson.

1. The 10 Vocabulary Upgrade Ladders Reference Matrix

Tier 1 is acceptable for casual chat with familiar peers. In cross-functional status meetings, formal emails, and client discussions, systematically deploy Tier 3.

# Tier 1 (Casual Default) Tier 2 (Functional Standard) Tier 3 (Executive Precision) & Register Notes
1 Do the project Carry out the project Execute / Implement
Execute a strategy / migration (rigorous operational delivery). Implement changes / policies (deploying into active use).
2 Make a plan Create / Build a plan Formulate / Architect
Formulate implies structured strategic thinking; architect implies designing a resilient, scalable solution.
3 Big problem Serious issue / Trouble Critical bottleneck
Identifies the specific constraint restricting system throughput, replacing vague panic words like toraburu.
4 Fix the bug Solve / Sort out Troubleshoot / Rectify
Troubleshoot = methodically investigate root cause. Rectify = restore errors, data discrepancies, or oversights to correct state.
5 Make work faster Speed up / Improve Streamline operations
Systematically eliminating redundancies and operational friction rather than merely rushing.
6 Think it will grow Expect / Believe Anticipate / Forecast
Anticipate = proactive readiness for expected events. Forecast = systematic forward projection based on data.
7 Talk about the budget Discuss the budget Deliberate on / Address
Deliberate on = formal committee discussion before deciding. Address = tackle directly (no preposition).
8 Give work to someone Pass over / Hand off Delegate / Assign
Delegate [task] to [person] (entrusting responsibility). Assign [task] to [person] (allocating ownership).
9 Lower the bad effects Reduce damage Mitigate risks
Standard risk-governance terminology to describe preventative action reducing downside exposure.
10 Check that we agree Make sure we agree Align on expectations
Establishes shared clarity on deliverables and timelines without sounding confrontational or clumsy.
2. Grammatical Precision & L1 Interference Guide Syntax Rules

Review these four structural rules where Japanese business speakers most frequently experience grammatical interference.

Rule 1: Direct Transitive Verbs (No Preposition) Syntax
The verbs address, mitigate, formulate, and rectify are direct transitive verbs. They take their object immediately without a preposition.
Trap: Do not say "address about the problem" or "rectify with the error". Say: "address the problem" and "rectify the error".
Rule 2: "Deliberate" Requires "On" Preposition
Unlike discuss (which takes no preposition), deliberate requires the preposition on (or occasionally over) when indicating the topic under consideration.
Trap: Say: "The board deliberated on the acquisition." (Not "deliberated the acquisition" or "deliberated about").
Rule 3: "Align On" vs. "Align With" Preposition
You align on a deliverable, roadmap, timeline, or expectation (the shared topic). You align with a stakeholder, team, client, or policy (the counterpart).
Trap: "Let's align on the deadline with the client."
Rule 4: Eliminating "Suru" & "Toraburu" Loanword Purge
Japanese relies heavily on general-purpose verbs (suru / do) and loanwords (toraburu / trouble). In English, state the exact operational reality: bottleneck, latency spike, discrepancy.
Trap: Replace "We had big trouble with the server" with "We hit a critical bottleneck during database synchronization."

Part B: Preposition Key & Grammatical Rationale
  • 1. on — deliberate on is the formal standard. Avoid deliberate about.
  • 2. NONE — address is a direct transitive verb. Never use address about.
  • 3. on — align on a deliverable, timeline, or scope.
  • 4. with — align with a third party, department, or standard.
  • 5. to — delegate responsibilities to a designated person.
  • 6. NONE — mitigate risks (direct object; no preposition in business English).
  • 7. in — a bottleneck occurs in a process, stage, or workflow.
  • 8. NONE — formulate a response (transitive verb; no up or out).
  • 9. NONE — rectify the discrepancies (direct transitive verb; no with).
  • 10. NONE — forecasts a 25% decrease (direct transitive verb; no for).
